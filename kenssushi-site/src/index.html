The story

Five lines on a strip of paper

When Grandma Setsu handed Ken her knife, she gave him five lines to live by. Each line is one of these games.

Read the full story

Ken grew up above his grandmother’s sushi counter in a small harbour town in Ise-Shima, where the forests of Mie run down to the sea. Before she opened the counter, Grandma Setsu was an ama, one of the women who dive for abalone off Ise-Shima on a single breath. Every morning she woke Ken before dawn with the same words: “Every plate starts long before the plate.”

The day she handed him her knife, she also gave him a strip of paper with five lines in her handwriting. Ken pinned it above the conveyor belt of his own little restaurant, and he still reads it every morning.

Each game follows one line, in the order a fish travels. It starts on Captain Kasumi’s boat, goes through the market and Ken’s new restaurant, rides the belt, and ends in the lunchbox Ken packs for Kasumi’s child, Minato.

The five games

From the sea to the lunchbox

Each game stands on its own. Played in order, they tell Ken’s story.

  1. Chapter 1The boat海 umi · the sea

    KEN’SMORNING CATCH

    “Respect the sea. Take only what it can spare.”

    Tie the floats into one strong net before the sun comes up.

    Coming later

    Story and how it plays

    Before sunrise Ken rides out with his school friend, Captain Kasumi, on the Kibō Maru, and they watch the sun come up over the water. Kasumi’s nets only take what the town needs, and the young fish go back. Tie the floats together so the net holds.

    How it plays. Join floats with straight ropes. Each float shows how many ropes it needs, ropes never cross, and the whole net must hold together.

  2. Chapter 2The market市 ichi · the market

    KEN’SFISH MARKET BIDS

    “Fight for your living, but fight fair.”

    Fill the auction board with fair bids that add up to the price.

    Coming later

    Story and how it plays

    At the Tsukiji market the auctioneer rings his bell and the bidding is a fight. Ken has been up since three. He bids hard for every crate, but he never cheats a fisher and never goes back on his word.

    How it plays. Each row and column uses every number once. Each crate of bids must add, subtract, multiply or divide to the price the auctioneer calls.

  3. Chapter 3The restaurant店 mise · the shop

    KEN’SOPENING DAY

    “Don’t be afraid to fail. Try, learn, and try again.”

    Give every table its own stool before the doors open.

    Coming later

    Story and how it plays

    Ken’s first little counter closed after one winter. He wrote down everything that went wrong, and now he is trying something new for his town, sushi on a conveyor belt. Opening day is tomorrow, and the stools are still stacked by the door.

    How it plays. Place one stool beside each table. Stools never touch, even at the corners, and the numbers on the edges say how many stools go in each row and column.

  4. Chapter 4The belt回 kai · to turn

    KEN’SSUSHI

    “Find your ikigai, the reason to get up each morning.”

    Match fish pairs into sushi and serve every order before patience runs out.

    {{SUSHI_PLAY}}

    Story and how it plays

    Lunch rush. The belt turns and customers call their orders. Ken loves this work, he is good at it, and his town needs it. That is his ikigai. Make the plates they ask for, and don’t let any fish go to waste.

    How it plays. Match free fish tiles in pairs to make nigiri. Serve each customer before their patience runs out. Dig through the stack when the fish they want is buried.

  5. Chapter 5The lunchbox弁 ben · the lunchbox

    KEN’SBENTO LETTERS

    “Be there for your family and your friends.”

    Spell words from nori letters to write Minato’s lunchbox note.

    Coming later

    Story and how it plays

    When Captain Kasumi is away at sea for days, Ken packs a lunch for Kasumi’s child, Minato. On every lunchbox he leaves a note, spelled in letters cut from nori and laid on the rice.

    How it plays. Swipe across the letters on the rice ball to spell words. Each word fills a line of the note. Extra words earn bonus hints.

Pinned above the counter

Grandma Setsu’s note

Ken has read it every morning for twenty years. The lettering on every game’s logo is traced from her handwriting.

Finish all five games and the note is the last thing you see.

Meet the people (and the dog)
Kenichi Haru
The chef, Ken to everyone in town. Smiling eyes, a steady knife, and a ten-seat kaiten counter by the harbour.
Grandma Setsu
Ken’s grandmother, who taught him to cook. Before she opened her sushi counter she was an ama, one of the free divers of Ise-Shima. Her five lines are the five games, one line each.
Captain Kasumi
Ken’s school friend and skipper of the Kibō Maru, which means “hope”.
Katsu
Ken’s Shiba Inu, who naps by the restaurant door and turns up in every game. Make three good moves in a row and Katsu lends a paw.
Minato
Captain Kasumi’s child, whose name means “harbour”. Ken packs Minato’s lunch when Kasumi is away at sea, and Minato reads the nori notes out loud at school.
The auctioneer
Runs the bidding at the fish market in Ken’s Fish Market Bids, and rings a brass hand bell to open every crate.
